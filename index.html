Finding content gaps with persistent homology, not just keyword clustering

A method note, with a working demo

Most content-cluster maps are built the same way: embed a pile of topics or keywords, run k-means or eyeball a 2D projection, and draw pillar pages around whatever blobs show up. That works for finding clusters. It's much weaker at finding gaps — topics that are clearly implied by everything around them but aren't directly covered by anything. Topological data analysis gives a more principled way to find those gaps, and to tell the real ones from noise.

The part standard clustering misses

K-means (and most clustering) answers "which points belong together?" It doesn't naturally answer a different, useful question: "is there a hole in the middle of this structure?" If four topic clusters sit roughly in a ring — each connected to its neighbors, but nothing occupying the center — that center is exactly where a bridge or hub topic belongs. Clustering alone won't surface it, because no single cluster contains it; it's defined by the shape the clusters make together, not by any one cluster's contents.

The tool: persistent homology, briefly

Topological data analysis treats a set of points (topics, keywords, anything with a distance between pairs) as a shape to be studied at every scale simultaneously, rather than committing to one clustering threshold up front. The standard construction:

  1. Start with a point cloud — each point is a topic, positioned so that distance between points reflects dissimilarity (a text-embedding space, for instance).
  2. Build a filtration. For a scale value ε, connect every pair of points within distance ε with an edge, and fill in a triangle wherever all three pairwise distances are within ε. This is the Vietoris–Rips complex at scale ε. As ε grows from 0 to ∞, the complex grows with it — more edges, more filled triangles.
  3. Track topological features across the whole sweep. At each ε, count connected components (β₀ — how many separate clusters exist right now) and independent loops (β₁ — how many "holes" exist that a triangle hasn't filled in). Each feature has a birth scale (when it first appears) and a death scale (when it merges away or gets filled in).
  4. Persistence is the signal. A feature that's born and dies almost immediately is noise — two nearly-identical points, or a stray triangle. A feature that survives across a wide range of ε is structurally real. That's the whole idea: don't pick one threshold, watch what survives most thresholds.

β₀ across the sweep gives you a full hierarchical clustering for free — which topics merge into which pillars, at every granularity, instead of committing to one cluster count. β₁ is the less obvious payoff: a long-lived loop means a set of topics forms a ring with nothing filling its center — a structurally real gap, not just a visually empty patch on a 2D plot.

Reading the demo as a content decision

Treat each point above as a topic already covered somewhere in a content library. At low ε (a strict similarity threshold), everything is its own island — β₀ is high, nothing is connected, which just means the topics are all somewhat distinct. As ε relaxes, adjacent topics connect first, and the ring closes into one component: β₀ drops to 1, and — critically — β₁ turns on. That's the moment the shape stops being "a bunch of related topics" and becomes "a ring with a hole in it." The hole is a topic that sits conceptually in the center of everything already covered, addressed by none of it directly.

Keep relaxing ε and the ring eventually gets chords across it — β₁ drops back to 0. That's the point where the gap stops being real: at a loose enough similarity threshold, everything looks connected to everything, and the "hole" was just an artifact of being too strict. The gap worth acting on is the one that stays open across a wide band of ε, not one that appears for a single threshold and vanishes at the next.

A real pipeline, not just the toy version

The demo above uses 2D pixel coordinates so the shape is visible. A real pipeline swaps that point cloud for actual content:

1. Embed each topic/keyword with a text-embedding model
   → a vector per topic, distance = semantic dissimilarity

2. Compute the pairwise distance matrix

3. Build the Vietoris–Rips filtration over that distance matrix
   (ripser, GUDHI, or giotto-tda handle this directly)

4. Compute persistent homology → a persistence diagram
   - long-lived β0 classes  → natural pillar/cluster boundaries,
     at whatever granularity you cut the filtration
   - long-lived β1 classes  → candidate structural gaps —
     inspect the representative cycle to see which topics
     ring the hole, and interpret what belongs in the middle

5. Cross-reference candidate gaps against demand data
   (search volume, ticket volume, whatever signal you have) —
   topology tells you where structure is, not whether
   anyone is asking about it
What this method doesn't do

Three honest limits, in the spirit of not overclaiming a technique:

  • The embedding is a modeling choice, not ground truth. Different embedding models or distance metrics will surface different topology from the same underlying topics. The output describes the shape of your chosen representation, not some objective structure of the subject matter.
  • A structural gap isn't automatically worth filling. Persistent homology finds where the shape has a hole. It says nothing about whether anyone searches for what belongs there — that still has to come from demand data, checked separately.
  • The demo above simplifies one thing worth naming directly: it renders a Betti curve (β₀ and β₁ plotted as step functions across the ε sweep) rather than a full persistence diagram with paired birth/death generators. That's a standard, legitimate simplification — it's derived from the same boundary-matrix computation (rank over GF(2), via β₀ = V − rank(∂₁) and β₁ = E − rank(∂₁) − rank(∂₂)) and gives an honest picture of when features are born and die, just without assigning each individual generator its own tracked lifetime.
